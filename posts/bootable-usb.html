How to Make a Bootable USB Drive

November 4, 2026

To install or try a Linux distro, you need a bootable USB drive. Here are the three most common ways to make one, and the mistake to avoid: writing to the wrong disk.

Before you start

  • Use a USB drive of 8 GB or more.
  • Back up anything on the drive. Every method below erases it.
  • Download the image from the official site and verify its SHA-256 checksum first.

Option 1: Rufus (Windows)

Rufus is a small Windows tool and the easiest option for most people.

  1. Plug in the USB drive and open Rufus.
  2. Under Device, check that the drive shown is your USB stick.
  3. Click SELECT and pick your ISO file.
  4. Leave the defaults unless the distro's site says otherwise, then click START.
  5. If Rufus asks about ISO or DD mode, ISO mode is usually fine.

Option 2: Ventoy (Windows and Linux)

Ventoy is handy if you want several distros on one drive.

  1. Install Ventoy onto the USB drive once.
  2. Copy any number of ISO files onto the drive like normal files.
  3. Boot from the drive and pick which ISO to start from the menu.

Installing Ventoy erases the drive, but after that, adding or removing ISOs does not.

Option 3: dd (Linux and macOS)

dd is built in and needs no extra software, but it has no safety net. Find the correct device first:

lsblk

Look for the device whose size matches your USB stick, for example /dev/sdb. Use the whole device, not a partition such as sdb1. Then run:

sudo dd if=distro.iso of=/dev/sdX bs=4M status=progress conv=fsync

Replace sdX with your device name. Triple-check it. If you point of= at your main disk, dd will erase it without asking.

Safety checklist

  • Unplug other USB drives and external disks before you start.
  • Confirm the drive by size and name every time.
  • Wait for the tool to finish and eject the drive safely before removing it.
  • If the PC does not boot from the USB, open the boot menu (often F12, F10, or Esc) and pick the drive. Secure Boot settings can also matter, so check the distro's documentation.

Balena Etcher is another simple option for Windows, macOS, and Linux if you prefer a point-and-click tool with a built-in verification step.
